# 2026 Colorado Eviction Laws: A Guide for Denver Landlords

Navigating the eviction process is one of the most stressful—and legally perilous—situations a landlord can face. In Colorado, tenant rights are robust, and the legal landscape surrounding evictions (formally known as Forcible Entry and Detainer, or FED) is highly specific. 

If you own rental property in Denver, Aurora, or anywhere along the Front Range, making a procedural mistake during an eviction can result in your case being thrown out of court, forcing you to restart the entire timeline while losing thousands of dollars in unpaid rent.

Here is a high-level guide to understanding the 2026 Colorado eviction laws, and why partnering with a top-rated property manager is your best defense.

## The Most Common Reasons for Eviction in Colorado

Under Colorado law, landlords cannot simply evict a tenant because they feel like it. You must have "just cause." The most common legal grounds for eviction include:

1. **Nonpayment of Rent:** The tenant fails to pay rent on time.
2. **Lease Violations:** The tenant violates a specific clause in the lease (e.g., unauthorized pets, subletting, excessive noise).
3. **Substantial Lease Violations:** The tenant commits a severe act, such as a violent crime or selling illegal drugs on the property.

## The Eviction Timeline and Notice Requirements

The most critical part of the Colorado eviction process is serving the correct initial notice. If you serve the wrong notice, or serve it incorrectly, the judge will dismiss your case.

### 1. The 10-Day Demand for Compliance or Right to Possession
In the vast majority of cases (for standard residential leases), if a tenant fails to pay rent or violates a standard lease term, the landlord must serve a **10-Day Notice**. 
*   **What it means:** The tenant has exactly 10 days to either pay the past-due rent (or fix the lease violation) or move out. 
*   *Note:* For employer-provided housing or certain exempt properties, this may be a 5-day notice, but 10 days is the standard for almost all standard residential rentals in 2026.

### 2. The 3-Day Notice for Substantial Violations
If the tenant commits a severe crime on the property, the landlord can serve a 3-Day Notice to Quit. In this scenario, the tenant does not have the option to "fix" the behavior; they must vacate within 3 days.

### 3. Filing the Eviction Lawsuit (FED)
If the notice period expires and the tenant has not complied or vacated, the landlord must officially file a Forcible Entry and Detainer (FED) lawsuit in the county court where the property is located (e.g., Denver County Court).

### 4. The Court Hearing and Writ of Restitution
If the judge rules in favor of the landlord, the court will issue a judgment for possession. However, the landlord still cannot physically remove the tenant. The court will issue a **Writ of Restitution**, which must be executed by the local Sheriff's Department. Under Colorado law, the Sheriff must wait at least 48 hours after the judgment before physically removing the tenant.

## Why DIY Evictions are Dangerous in Denver

Attempting a "self-help" eviction—such as changing the locks, shutting off utilities, or removing the tenant's belongings yourself—is strictly illegal in Colorado. If you attempt this, the tenant can sue you for significant financial damages.

Furthermore, local municipalities (like the City of Denver) often enact specific local ordinances that add layers of complexity on top of state law.
