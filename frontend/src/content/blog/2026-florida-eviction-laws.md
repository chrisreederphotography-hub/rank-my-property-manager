# Florida Eviction Laws 2026: The 3-Day Notice Explained for Miami Landlords

Owning rental property in Miami, Florida can be incredibly lucrative, but dealing with a tenant who refuses to pay rent can quickly drain your profits. When a landlord-tenant relationship breaks down in Miami-Dade County, understanding the precise legal steps required for a legal eviction is absolutely critical. 

In Florida, the eviction process is fast, but it is strictly governed by state statutes. One minor procedural error—like writing the wrong date on a notice—can cause a judge to throw out your case, forcing you to start over.

Here is a high-level guide to understanding Florida’s standard eviction process for non-payment of rent, and why hiring a professional Miami property manager is your best defense against prolonged vacancies.

## The Foundation: The 3-Day Notice to Pay or Vacate

The most common reason for eviction in Florida is the non-payment of rent. Unlike some states that require a 10-day or 14-day waiting period, Florida law allows landlords to move quickly. 

When a tenant fails to pay rent when it is due, the landlord must serve a **3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Vacate**.

### How to Calculate the 3 Days
This is where many DIY landlords make a fatal mistake. When calculating the three days, you **cannot** include:
1. The day the notice is delivered.
2. Weekends (Saturday and Sunday).
3. Legal court holidays.

For example, if rent is due on the 1st (a Tuesday), and it is not paid, you serve the 3-Day Notice on Wednesday the 2nd. The three days begin counting on Thursday the 3rd. Thursday is Day 1, Friday is Day 2, the weekend does not count, and Monday is Day 3. The tenant has until the end of the day on Monday to pay the rent in full or vacate.

### What Must Be Included in the Notice?
The notice must clearly state:
*   The exact amount of rent owed (do not include late fees or utility charges unless your lease specifically defines them as "additional rent").
*   The date the notice was served.
*   A clear statement demanding payment or possession within three business days.

## Filing the Eviction (Unlawful Detainer)

If the 3-Day Notice expires and the tenant has neither paid nor moved out, you can officially file an eviction lawsuit (often called an action for possession) in the Miami-Dade County court. 

The court will issue a Summons and Complaint to the tenant. The tenant then has **5 days** (again, excluding weekends and legal holidays) to file a written response with the court. Furthermore, to contest the eviction, the tenant is typically required to deposit the disputed rent amount into the court registry.

## The Final Step: The Writ of Possession

If the tenant fails to respond, or if the judge rules in your favor at a hearing, the court will issue a judgment for possession. 

**Important:** You still cannot physically remove the tenant, change the locks, or shut off their utilities. Attempting a "self-help" eviction in Florida is illegal and can subject you to severe financial damages. 

Instead, the clerk will issue a **Writ of Possession** to the Miami-Dade Police Department (or local sheriff). The police will post a 24-hour warning on the tenant's door, after which they will physically remove the tenant and restore the property to you.
